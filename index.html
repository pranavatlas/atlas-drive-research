03 / Motion outcome

Your motion outcome matters just as much as your actuator design.

Even though the design of an actuator can dramatically change the performance of a robot, the same actuator, with the same gains and timing, can still perform differently based on its “motion outcome.”

One example of what we call “motion outcome” is the following. When we built Atlas Drive, we trained a decision tree to pick a withdrawal motion for a given robot and object state. Our decision tree reduced the worst observed object speed by 24.2% when we tested it across 64 loaded cases. The worst observed speed fell from 0.05978 to 0.04530 m/s.

Fresh simulation; secondary result from a tree frozen before testing. The 64 varied cases reuse eight loaded geometries across eight engineering blocks; another 16 cases check retention. The primary neural candidate completed 79/80 and failed its gates. This comparison does not isolate a benefit from motor-model fidelity or establish hardware transfer.

05 / Build with Atlas

Building custom actuators
from your unique tasks.

Making custom actuators with Atlas Drive has three steps.

  1. 01

    Bring the workload.

    Bring your robot, with information about its motions, payload, space, power, and duty cycle.

  2. 02

    Review the recommendation.

    Atlas will generate specifications for you to review: how your design performs on the task, where its limits are, and the tradeoffs that matter for your product.

  3. 03

    Build with Atlas.

    Atlas will do the detailed actuator engineering and manufacturing for you. You’ll get the hardware along with its controls model.

Our model runtime is open source, so you can drop it into your robotics stack today. When it’s time to build an actuator, bring the job to Atlas and we’ll manufacture the motor.

Example appendix +

What the arm result shows.

In our recorded arm trials, three hypothetical actuator designs ran the same controller. Payload, timing, and reach determine which designs can finish the task, although some jobs are out of reach for all of them. These are simulated retrofits rather than qualified hardware.

Where training and bench evidence fit.

In our simulations, the trained selector shows a specific benefit over fixed motion curves. Our separate bench comparison shows an improvement from measured calibration. Neither proves a training advantage unique to our motor model or validates an arm retrofit on real hardware. We keep the full records internal, including failed gates.
